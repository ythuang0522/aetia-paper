% Discussion. Interpretation, limitations and prospective evaluation.
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

Respiratory \mngs findings required organism-level clinical interpretation. In the registry, 63\% of reported organisms were not adjudicated pathogens, read count ranked a pathogen above a non-pathogen in only 61\% of pairs and ten species were causative in some patients but not in others. Clinicians nevertheless attributed an antimicrobial change to \mngs in 49.5\% of the registry, including 13 of 19 de-escalations. The patient's subsequent course did not indicate whether the organism treated was the right one: every \kmuh patient febrile before the specimen was afebrile by day 3--7 and oxygenation improved in 17 of 27 patients who nevertheless died, so the bedside signals read as confirmation of an empirical choice occurred in survivors and decedents alike and reversed in roughly half. In the 55-patient evaluation cohort, \toolname combined modality-specific extraction, deterministic scoring, safety review and case-matched literature to achieve 91.4\% recall and 71.6\% precision. It retained the sensitivity of the unfiltered \mngs report while substantially improving precision and outperformed individual conventional read-outs. On 41 patients, it also exceeded four general-purpose language models prompted directly with the same data by 13.6--15.1 F1 points. These findings support structured integration of sequencing, conventional microbiology and clinical data rather than interpretation by a single assay threshold or model prompt.

The comparison with direct prompting suggests that the main advantage was task structure rather than model capacity. The direct prompts specified colonization, anatomical-site and negative-control considerations, and their precision was similar to that of \toolname. Their lower recall indicates that general caution was applied too broadly. A deterministic rule can encode organism-specific exceptions, such as lower read thresholds for protected pathogens or additional requirements for environmental organisms. The literature stage then evaluates whether published cases match the species, site, syndrome and host. \todo{Replace the proposed \textit{Stenotrophomonas} and \textit{Pneumocystis} examples with verified cases from the per-patient comparison.} The small recall difference among the four directly prompted models further suggests that increasing model capacity alone may not resolve this formulation problem.

Traceability is a separate contribution. The clinical records contained many discordant and asynchronous results: a median of 247 recorded results and eight named organisms per admission, with only 19\% of organism--admission pairs supported by more than one read-out. \toolname records the evidence supporting or opposing every selected organism and the reason each non-selected organism was excluded. Because the structured decision is fixed before the clinical rationale is generated, the rationale can be checked against the rule path and source values. This differs from a model-generated account produced in the same pass as the prediction, which may not reflect the computation that determined the answer\cite{turpin2023language} and can provide false reassurance in clinical use\cite{ghassemi2021false}. This design does not establish that the rationales are adequate for clinical use, but it makes discrepancies identifiable and testable.

The study has several limitations. It was retrospective, included 55 patients in the primary analysis and 41 in the model comparison, and used data from three hospitals in one country and one sequencing laboratory. The reference standard was physician adjudication informed by the \mngs report and was therefore not independent of the index test. The rule set was developed with access to these labels, no patient was held out for external validation and the reported performance is a development-set estimate. Both evaluation cohorts required at least one adjudicated pathogen at a pulmonary site, so patients whose final diagnosis was infection elsewhere or no infection were excluded. This is a selection on the outcome: it defines a population that cannot be identified when the test is ordered, and it removes the patients in whom the site and colonization guardrails would be most consequential, since the correct output for them is an empty pulmonary list. The precision reported here therefore applies to patients in whom pneumonia was ultimately confirmed and should not be read as the false-positive rate a clinician would encounter when sending \mngs on suspicion. This missing stratum is clinically relevant: 22 of 105 registry specimens had no recorded causative pathogen, although the interpretation of 16 unlabelled entries requires confirmation. Scoring \toolname and the comparators in these patients is an important next analysis.

The pipeline also shares constraints with the underlying evidence. An organism detected only in a non-sterile specimen cannot be proven causative without corroborating clinical or microbiological findings. Guardrails that correctly remove most airway colonizers may also demote uncommon true infections, including \textit{Candida} pneumonia, herpes simplex virus tracheobronchitis or galactomannan-negative aspergillosis. Conversely, 31\% of adjudicated pathogens were absent from the \mngs report of the sequenced specimen; the pipeline can recover such pathogens only when another test identifies them. These errors require an additional specimen or assay, not greater sequencing depth. The literature and case-fit stages use a commercial language model and a changing PubMed corpus. Frozen article snapshots permit replay of the reported analysis, but future runs may retrieve different evidence and model judgements are not deterministic. The two illustrative cases originated from an earlier 35-patient series and require confirmation using the delivered records from the primary cohort. Finally, we assessed agreement with adjudication rather than clinical benefit. We did not determine whether the selected lists changed antimicrobial treatment or outcomes, nor did independent physicians assess the completeness, fidelity or usefulness of the clinical rationales. The registry does record what the clinicians did next, and acting on the sequencing report was not associated with survival in it; that null is not evidence against benefit, because every registry patient was sequenced, the exposure is the treating team's own retrospective attribution rather than an assignment, and no antimicrobial agent or date is recorded from which the appropriateness of a change or the time to appropriate therapy could be computed. The higher mortality among patients whose treatment changed measures the indication for changing it rather than its effect, and any endpoint measured in days is subject to immortal time, because a change credited to a specimen taken days into an admission can only be recorded in patients who survive to receive it. The trajectory analysis sharpens this gap without closing it. It establishes that the window is long---death followed the specimen by a median of 15 days---and that the response signals available at the bedside do not discriminate, but it is observational, every patient was sequenced, and antipyretics, corticosteroids, sedation and ventilator settings act directly on temperature and gas exchange and were not recorded in a form permitting adjustment. The temperature column of the whole-record extracts also required repair before use (Methods).

A prospective study should freeze the rules and prompts before enrolment, include patients without infection, use an adjudication committee blinded to the pipeline output and evaluate changes in antimicrobial treatment and clinical outcomes. Two analyses of the existing cohort should precede it. The \kmuh discharge records contain the antimicrobial charts, so the interval from the report to a change of therapy and the adequacy of that change against the adjudicated pathogen are extractable without enrolling anyone; and the 22 specimens without a recorded pathogen provide the no-infection stratum once their adjudication convention is confirmed. The quality of the clinical rationale should be assessed independently by comparing decisions made with and without the evidence record and by auditing exclusion reasons in false-negative cases. The same architecture could incorporate resistance-gene evidence and be evaluated in bloodstream and central nervous system infections. Such extensions should follow external validation of the present pathogen-selection rules.
