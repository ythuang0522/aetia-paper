\subsection*{\toolname separates evidence extraction, rule-based selection and literature-grounded rescue}

\toolname processes nine clinical sources for each patient: admission diagnosis, underlying conditions, blood counts, other laboratory values, cultures, \filmarray, galactomannan, molecular microbiology and imaging, together with the laboratory \mngs report (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}a). A dedicated language-model agent converts each clinical source to a fixed schema. Each agent receives one modality and no reference labels. A deterministic summary combines the outputs into organism-specific evidence and host context.

Selection is rule-based (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}b). Each organism on the \mngs report is graded by its signal relative to negative controls, relative and absolute read burden, within-specimen dominance, anatomical alignment and support from hospital tests. These features and host immunosuppression determine a five-level causative ranking (Methods and Supplementary Tables~1 and~2). Prespecified guardrails demote common colonizers, oral flora, environmental organisms, skin flora, low-actionability herpesviruses, weak mould signals and non-specific laboratory labels. Organisms in the top two levels are selected; intermediate organisms require protected-pathogen status or direct support. A language-model safety review flags unselected organisms for further assessment but cannot alter or reorder the selected list.

For each selected or flagged candidate, the pipeline assembles literature, sequencing and clinical evidence (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}c). Up to ten PubMed articles are assessed first for species-specific evidence of infection at the target site and then for similarity to the patient's specimen, syndrome, host, phenotype and timing. Separate modules quantify absolute and relative sequencing strength, exact-organism support from non-sequencing tests and colonization risk. Missing or pending tests remain unknown. The final rule retains every deterministic selection and adds a candidate only when the same organism is isolated from a sterile site or tissue, or when case-matched literature, site alignment and a qualifying sequencing signal are all present (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}d). Explanation generation begins only after this list has been fixed. The delivered table and traceable files report the evidence and rule associated with each selected and excluded organism (Fig.~\ref{fig:explain}e).

\subsection*{\toolname preserves pathogen recall while improving precision over individual tests}

The primary evaluation included 55 patients with suspected lower respiratory tract infection from Kaohsiung Medical University Hospital (\kmuh, $n=30$), Taipei Veterans General Hospital (\tvgh) and Tri-Service General Hospital (\tsgh; combined $n=25$), drawn from the 105-specimen registry (Table~\ref{tab:cohort}; Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:ed1}). At sampling, 71\% were in intensive care, 56\% had hospital- or ventilator-associated pneumonia, 37\% had an active malignancy, 39\% were receiving systemic corticosteroids and 60\% had bronchoalveolar-lavage specimens. Treating infectious-diseases physicians provided the reference pathogen lists. Performance was evaluated at the exact-species (patient, organism) level using micro-averaged precision, recall and F1.

\toolname achieved 71.6\% precision, 91.4\% recall and 80.3\% F1 (Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmark}a,b). The unfiltered \mngs report had similar recall (93.1\%) but 8.0\% precision (F1 14.8\%). \filmarray and galactomannan testing had 67.6\% precision and 39.7\% recall (F1 50.0\%), whereas culture had 48.1\% precision and 43.1\% recall (F1 45.5\%). Thus, \toolname retained most of the sensitivity of the unfiltered report while increasing precision approximately ninefold.

In a separate, earlier series of 35 \kmuh intensive-care patients analysed with the first pipeline version, culture reported at least one organism in 24\% of patients and \filmarray in 64\%, whereas \mngs reported at least one organism in every patient (Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmark}c,d). The mean number of organisms per patient was 5.76 for \mngs and 1.97 after pipeline processing. The overlap of this prototype series with the primary cohort remains to be established (Methods).

\subsection*{\toolname achieves higher recall than directly prompted general-purpose models}

We compared \toolname with four general-purpose models (\gptsol, \gptluna, \gptterra and \gptastra) in 41 patients (\kmuh $n=30$, \tvgh and \tsgh $n=11$). Each model received the same de-identified \mngs and clinical data as \toolname, one expert-written prompt and a structured output schema, without retrieval, a supplied candidate list or reference labels (Methods and Supplementary Note~2). Predictions were stored before evaluation.

The four directly prompted models had precision of 67.7--70.5\%, recall of 56.6--60.5\% and F1 of 62.4--63.9\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:llm}). In the same 41 patients, \toolname achieved 67.7\% precision, 90.8\% recall and 77.5\% F1 (69 true positives, 33 false positives and 7 false negatives), exceeding the directly prompted models by 13.6--15.1 F1 points. \subsection*{\toolname records supporting evidence for 123 selections and reasons for 395 exclusions}

The source records were multimodal and longitudinal. Among 32 \kmuh admissions with available source workbooks, each admission contained a median of 247 recorded results (IQR 214--263; range 157--423) across laboratory testing, imaging and microbiology during a 31-day period, with new results on a median of 16 days and 1,452 words of free text (Fig.~\ref{fig:explain}a--c). The records named a median of eight organisms per admission (IQR 6--11). Of 270 organism--admission pairs, 81\% were named by only one read-out and 91\% were never isolated from a sterile site. Among 365 chest-imaging studies, 90\% mentioned pneumonia, infiltrate or consolidation, but 44\% of these reports qualified the finding (Fig.~\ref{fig:explain}d). Culture reports with no growth were issued after a median of 6 days, compared with 3 days for reports with growth.

Across the 55-patient evaluation, the deterministic rules considered 518 candidates and selected 114; the final evidence rule added nine, yielding 123 selected candidates (2.2 per patient) and 395 exclusions (Fig.~\ref{fig:explain}h). These nine additions could arise either from an exact-organism sterile-site or tissue isolate or from case-matched literature combined with site alignment and a qualifying sequencing signal. Each delivered record reports the selection or exclusion rule, supporting and opposing findings, specimen site and timing, sequencing burden and dominance, and any matched PubMed articles (Fig.~\ref{fig:explain}e--g). \subsection*{Airway \mngs reports contain more organisms but have limited corroboration across tests}

The three-hospital registry contained 105 \mngs specimens (Table~\ref{tab:cohort}; Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:ed2}). Airway specimens contained a median of five reported organisms (IQR 3--7; $n=67$), compared with one (IQR 1--2; $n=38$) in blood, cerebrospinal fluid, tissue and other sterile-site specimens. Apparent differences by illness severity disappeared after restriction to bronchoalveolar lavage: the median was five organisms in intensive care and on the ward, 5.5 with corticosteroid treatment and five without it, and five with and without acute respiratory distress syndrome. The difference between hospital- or ventilator-associated and community-acquired pneumonia persisted within lavage specimens (five versus three organisms). Thus, the number of organisms requiring interpretation was associated primarily with sampling an airway compartment.

Conventional tests corroborated a minority of reported organisms (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:ed2}b). Of 414 organisms on the 105 \mngs reports, 75 (18.1\%) had a same-species culture during the admission; conversely, 90 of 165 cultured organisms (54.5\%) were absent from \mngs. Among 312 registry comparisons, 49.0\% were sequencing detections without a culture counterpart, 29.8\% were partial matches, 6.7\% were mismatches and 5.8\% were exact matches, using the registry's categories. Viruses accounted for 27.1\% of reported organisms and 21.8\% of adjudicated pathogens, and 100 of 112 reported viruses were herpesviruses.

Adjudicated pathogen lists were available in the shared workbook for a separate subset of 33 registry cases (Supplementary Note~6). Their \mngs reports contained 104 organisms, of which 38 (37\%) were adjudicated as causative and 66 (63\%) as colonizing, contaminating or incidental. Read count ranked a causative organism above a non-causative organism in only 61\% of within-case pairs, and the distributions overlapped across five orders of magnitude. Ten species were causative in one patient and non-causative in another. For example, \textit{Acinetobacter nosocomialis} with 189,467 reads in lavage fluid was adjudicated as colonization, whereas \textit{Pneumocystis jirovecii} with 7 reads was adjudicated as causative.

The same 33-case analysis contained 55 adjudicated pathogens, 17 (31\%) of which were absent from the \mngs report of the sequenced specimen. Another read-out identified 16 of these 17 pathogens: culture in eight, PCR in five, galactomannan in two and IgM serology in one. Fourteen were identified within two days of \mngs sampling, and 11 were identified in a different specimen type. Sequencing and conventional tests therefore had complementary failure modes.

The registry also contained 22 specimens (21.0\%) without a recorded causative pathogen. Six had an explicit negative entry, whereas 16 had the dash used by one hospital for unlabelled rows. \mngs reported at least one organism in 18 of these 22 specimens, with 47 organisms in total and as many as eight in one specimen. \todo{Confirm with the \tvgh investigators whether the dash in the adjudication column means ``no causative pathogen identified'' or ``not recorded''; if the former, these 22 specimens are the no-infection stratum the evaluation cohorts lack, and the baselines and \toolname should be scored on them.}

\subsection*{Nearly half of patients had treatment changes attributed to \mngs without a detectable survival difference}

Antimicrobial treatment changed in 69 of the 105 registry patients (65.7\%), and clinicians attributed a change to \mngs in 52 (49.5\% of the registry; 58.4\% of the 89 patients with recorded attribution). These changes comprised 36 escalations and 13 de-escalations; 13 of all 19 de-escalations were attributed to \mngs. The cohort was severely ill: 82.7\% underwent mechanical ventilation within three days of sampling and 48.6\% died in hospital. Reports associated and not associated with an attributed treatment change contained the same median number of organisms (four).

No survival difference was detected between the 52 patients whose treatment change was attributed to \mngs and the 37 whose change was not. In-hospital death occurred in 30 of 52 patients (57.7\%) and 21 of 37 patients (56.8\%), respectively (odds ratio 1.04, 95\% confidence interval 0.45--2.41; $P = 1.00$, Fisher's exact test). Adjustment for intensive care at sampling, acute respiratory distress syndrome, active malignancy, corticosteroid treatment and age gave a similar estimate (adjusted odds ratio 1.22, 95\% confidence interval 0.46--3.21; $P = 0.69$). This retrospective attribution was not an assigned exposure, every patient underwent \mngs and the registry contained neither antimicrobial agents nor administration dates; the analysis therefore does not estimate a causal effect of \mngs-guided treatment or the appropriateness of therapy.

\subsection*{Early defervescence and improved oxygenation also occur in patients who subsequently die}

We examined physiological trajectories in 48 \kmuh admissions with machine-readable complete discharge records (31 deaths and 17 survivors), using every dated laboratory value and vital sign and anchoring observations to the day of the \mngs specimen (Methods) (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:ed3}). The analysis was descriptive because the records did not contain antimicrobial start dates, stop dates or coverage of the adjudicated pathogen.

Among the 31 patients who died, death occurred a median of 15 days after the \mngs specimen (IQR 7--21; range 1--57) (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:ed3}a); 22 died more than 7 days and 17 more than 14 days after sampling. Every patient who was febrile in the two weeks before sampling---10 decedents and 5 survivors---was afebrile on days 3--7. Among decedents, the proportion with a maximum temperature of at least 38\,\textdegree C fell from 39\% before sampling to 18\% on days 0--2 and 3\% on days 3--7. Temperature fell by a median of 0.5\,\textdegree C between the pre-sampling and day 3--7 windows ($n=27$ paired; $P = 0.001$, Wilcoxon signed-rank). Arterial $P_{\mathrm{O}_2}$ increased by days 3--7 in 17 of 27 decedents. These changes were often transient: fever returned in 9 of 21 decedents whose temperature improved, and $P_{\mathrm{O}_2}$ subsequently fell in 12 of the 17 whose oxygenation improved.

Platelet trajectories differed descriptively between survivors and decedents (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:ed3}c). Median platelet count in survivors increased from 169 before sampling to 257 on days 3--7 and 360 on days 8--14, whereas the corresponding values in decedents were 82, 48 and 55$\times10^{3}\,\mu$L$^{-1}$. Among 14 assessable decedents, the count declined by a median of 19$\times10^{3}\,\mu$L$^{-1}$ from the pre-sampling to day 3--7 window ($P = 0.004$) and improved in 2. Lactate, white-cell count, creatinine and procalcitonin did not show corresponding differences between the groups. These trajectories show that early defervescence and improved oxygenation were not confined to survivors; without treatment timing or coverage, they cannot establish whether the causative organism was correctly identified or treated.
